# Canonical locations: behind the wall

Generated 2026-08-01 from `roster.json`, so STYLE and NEGATIVE are the current blocks
verbatim rather than a copy that can go stale. **3:2 and the largest resolution available.**

Built the same way as the gym and the wardrobe room: empty space, nobody in it, wide enough
to hold the whole of it in one view, generated in the state the prose later changes, with a
MUST HOLD on whatever geometry a plate could contradict.

Two adjacent spaces, and they are a **matched pair**: the small room opens off the wardrobe
room, and a second door at the far end of the small room opens into the archive. They must
read as connected, so generate them in this order and attach the small room when generating
the archive.

**This is where the arc ends, and it is the only impossible space in either arc.** Everything
else Millbrook has shown the reader is an ordinary place that something strange happened to.
This is the one that is strange in itself, so it has to be underplayed: low, quiet, warm and
maintained. Nothing gothic, nothing vast-and-echoing, no shafts of light, no dust motes. The
unsettling part is that somebody keeps it tidy.

### It is a state pair, and the reference is BEFORE

The stamp is intact, in its cradle, warm enough that the air moves over it. Then the dragon
lands on it, gets a foot caught and takes the whole thing off the bench — and the last time
the room is seen, *“the workbench was empty and swept clean, with a folded square of paper
towel sitting in the middle of it and broken glass inside.”*

**So the reference must establish the bench, the cradle and the intact stamp**, because two
later plates depend on the reader recognising what is now missing. Same discipline as
`MAPLE_AND_FOURTH` A and B.

### The second door has to be there and has to be boring

It is the same trick the wardrobe room plays with the first door: *“He went to the back of
the room and opened the second door”* is the last turn in the arc, and it lands only if the
door was already in the room and the reader never looked at it. Present, closed, unremarkable.

### Scale is the risk here

Chet is **three feet tall** and the door is four and a half. The bench has to be a bench HE
works at, so it sits low, and the whole room should read as built slightly small — not as a
normal workshop that happens to have a short robot in it.

### One spread, and it is the last one

*“No light came out of it, and no wind, and no sound, and none of the things any of them had
braced for. It was a doorway, and past the doorway was a room, and the room was enormous.”*

**That sentence is a brief for what NOT to do.** The prose spends its whole first line ruling
out spectacle, so the plate cannot supply any. No glow spilling through the door, no wind, no
beams, no scale-drama. Just a room that is too big, lit low and gold, and calm.

### The chair is empty, and that is the whole discipline

The man sits in it — *“in a wooden chair much too big for him, with his feet nowhere near the
floor”* — but a canonical reference is a space with nobody in it, the rule that kept Ida’s
wave out of her sheet. **The chair being too big must be legible with nobody sitting in it**,
which is why it is specified as too large for an adult.

### “Bookshelves, or file drawers”

The prose deliberately does not decide, so the reference must not either. They should read as
both and resolve as neither. Nothing is labelled, which the negative block requires anyway and
which here is also the point: an archive you cannot index.

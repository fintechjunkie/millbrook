“The curfew isn’t Crane’s,” Milo said.

### The Spring Days Poster

Milo went alone, because Dalton is a one-person job. Monke came too, and nobody noticed until the elevator.

The Mayor’s office had a window unit and a whole wall of photographs of the Mayor shaking hands with people. Taped behind the door at eye level was the poster for Millbrook Spring Days, with MAY 12 across the middle and, underneath it, RIBBON CUTTING: MAYOR THOMAS DALTON.

“Seventy-two consecutive hours.”

“Run by the county administrator.” He took the 3D glasses off, which Milo had never seen him do. “Bill Rensch is the county administrator. Bill Rensch. That man owns a pontoon boat.”

### Two Fifteen

He called it for two fifteen on the Town Hall steps, with a lectern brought out and a flag behind it, and he wore the good suit. Lena set up behind the chairs, in her spot, where the light is good.

Crane stood off to the side with his hands behind his back. Twenty minutes earlier, on the receiver Felix had built out of a car stereo and most of a baby monitor, Owen had heard a radio channel go from four people talking to nobody talking at all. That is what a unit sounds like when it has just been told there is nothing it can do.

“My fellow citizens. Effective at five o’clock this afternoon, the curfew is lifted.”

One person in the back clapped and then stopped.

“I want to be clear that this decision was mine and mine alone, and that it came out of a close personal review of the county code, which I conducted myself.” He paused for applause, and it came in thin. “Millbrook is not a town that lives under a curfew. Millbrook is a town that lives in the light.”

Lena put her hand up, and she kept her face friendly and her voice easy, and she asked the only question she had come to ask.

“Mr. Mayor, can you walk us through how you figured it out?”

Then she did the hardest thing in her whole job, which is nothing at all.

## Spread 6

Pages 13 to 14 | Part Four: Declared Civil Contingency | Words 251

### Text page (left), verbatim

Mayor Dalton talked for four minutes and eleven seconds. He explained the seventy-two hour threshold, and he called it a Declared Civil Contingency Event three times and got it wrong twice. He explained that he had been fully prepared to hand the town over to the county for the good of his people, and that it was a testament to his vigilance that no such sacrifice was needed. He mentioned Spring Days twice. He described himself, out loud, as a student of municipal law.

Lena didn’t ask a follow-up. She didn’t ask which section it was and she didn’t ask him to spell it.

She thanked him, went home, and uploaded all four minutes and eleven seconds of it without a single cut, under the title MILLBROOK CURFEW LIFTED. FULL STATEMENT FROM MAYOR DALTON. By nine that night it had eleven thousand views in a town of four thousand people.

The comment section handled the rest. Somebody clipped student of municipal law and set it to music. Somebody else looked up Section 40-11.3, which turns out to be about licensing food trucks, and posted a screenshot, and that one got four hundred likes.

Lena didn’t reply to a single one of them. She sat on the Warehouse couch with the pig and watched a town that had brought lemonade to a roadblock spend six hours calling its own mayor a clown, in public, under its own name.

“I didn’t do that,” she said.

“No,” Milo said. “You just held the microphone.”

### The Thing On The Bench

They went down at midnight, all of them, because Ottie wouldn’t be talked out of it and nobody wanted to be the one who stayed home.

The wardrobe room was the same, and trunk seven was still open.

Behind the trunks, in the north wall, there was a door that didn’t appear on any drawing in any county file. It was about four and a half feet tall and it was standing open.

Inside was a room, and in the room was a robot.

He was maybe three feet tall, gray and scuffed and dented at one hip, with a head shaped like a bread box, two wide eyes set into it, and a spray of stiff blond hair coming off the top like somebody had glued a paintbrush up there. He was working at a bench and he didn’t stop when six people came through the door.

On the bench, sitting in a wooden cradle, was the thing that had been doing all of it. It looked like a library date stamp, brass and glass with a wooden handle worn pale, and it was warm enough that you could see the air moving over the top of it.

Pip made a sound none of them had ever heard her make. “It’s you.”

The robot turned around.

“Hi, Pip.” His voice came out of a speaker grille and there was nothing frightening in it at all. He sounded like a guy who comes to fix your furnace. “You got tall.”

He went to the back of the room and opened the second door.

No light came out of it, and no wind, and no sound, and none of the things any of them had braced for. It was a doorway, and past the doorway was a room, and the room was enormous. ### Coda

The camp was gone by Thursday, faster than it went up.

Lena posted two minutes that night, just her on the Warehouse couch with the camera propped against a coffee mug.

“Millbrook had an army in it for six days and then it didn’t. Four people showed up that nobody can explain and then they left. Nobody got hurt. One truck got destroyed. Our mayor is internet famous for a law that doesn’t exist, and he did that to himself, on camera, while I stood there and said nothing.”

She stopped for a second.

“Here’s what I keep thinking about, though. Everybody in this town knew something was wrong and everybody brought a casserole. I don’t think that’s stupid. I want to be careful about that, because I really don’t think it’s stupid. I think it’s what scared looks like after you’ve been scared for a long time.” A beat. “I’m not bringing a casserole. That’s the whole update. This is The Real Feed.”

Four thousand views by morning, and one of them came from a hotel that had already checked out.

Under a closed elementary school, in a small room behind a small door, the workbench was empty and swept clean, with a folded square of paper towel sitting in the middle of it and broken glass inside.

Up in the wardrobe room, the inventory sheet said two hundred and six items in a room that held two hundred and five.

Nobody had corrected it in eleven years, and nobody was going to.

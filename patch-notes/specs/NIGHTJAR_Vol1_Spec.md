### Rosa’s Truck

Rosa Delgado parked at the little league field on Tuesdays, and on Tuesdays half of Millbrook ate dinner standing up in a gravel lot.

She’d been doing it for four years out of a white truck with a hand-painted sign, and she knew everybody’s order, and she knew everybody’s mother. If you wanted to find somebody in this town on a Tuesday evening, you didn’t call them. You went and stood in her line.

The crew got there at six. Vex was already there, which is how it always works.

“You ordered without us,” Owen said.

“I ordered for all of you, and you can pay me back or you can starve.” Vex had one foot up on the bumper of somebody’s parked car. “Rosa, tell him what he’s having.”

“Two carnitas, no onion, because you don’t like the way they feel,” Rosa said through the window. “Milo, three al pastor. Lena, the fish, and yes, I know you always get the fish, and yes, I made extra for you.”

“You’re my favorite person in this town,” Lena said.

“I know that, mija.”

Felix was over at the side of the truck with his head under the awning, doing something to the generator with a screwdriver he had not asked permission to bring.

Nobody died and nobody nearly died. That should be said up front, because it was never that kind of night. It was the kind where you learn how thin the walls in your house are, and where eleven children discover the same thing at the same time in the same terrible detail.

Aunt Carol made a pot of something with rice in it that Lena would not describe afterward. Somebody put a message on the town’s emergency phone line, which nobody in Millbrook knew existed, saying that the situation was under control and that residents should stay hydrated and await further guidance. The Mayor’s name was on it four times.

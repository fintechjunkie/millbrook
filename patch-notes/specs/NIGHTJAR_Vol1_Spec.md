### Rosa’s Truck

Rosa Delgado parked at the little league field on Tuesdays, and on Tuesdays half of Millbrook ate dinner standing up in a gravel lot.

She’d been doing it for four years out of a white truck with a hand-painted sign, and she knew everybody’s order, and she knew everybody’s mother. If you wanted to find somebody in this town on a Tuesday evening, you didn’t call them. You went and stood in her line.

The crew got there at six. Vex was already there, which is how it always works.

“You ordered without us,” Owen said.

“I ordered for all of you, and you can pay me back or you can starve.” Vex had one foot up on the bumper of somebody’s parked car. “Rosa, tell him what he’s having.”

“Two carnitas, no onion, because you don’t like the way they feel,” Rosa said through the window. “Milo, three al pastor. Lena, the fish, and yes, I know you always get the fish, and yes, I made extra for you.”

“You’re my favorite person in this town,” Lena said.

“I know that, mija.”

Felix was over at the side of the truck with his head under the awning, doing something to the generator with a screwdriver he had not asked permission to bring.

“Felix, get out of there.”

“Rosa, your voltage is sagging. Every time your fryer kicks on, your lights dim. Do you hear that noise it makes? That’s not a noise a generator should make.”

“That noise is a warning. You want me to look at it properly?”

“That’s exactly right,” she said.

“I am usually right about this.”

“You come back Thursday, honey. I want to see you do that with the mole.”

The generator ran rough at seven o’clock and Rosa lost power for about an hour, which meant the warmer went cold and came back on again. Nobody thought anything about it. There were eleven people in line and she didn’t want to send them home.

Later on, every adult in Millbrook would have a theory about what happened, and the actual answer was that boring.

### Ninety-One

It started around midnight and it was in every house in town by two.

Nobody died and nobody nearly died. That should be said up front, because it was never that kind of night. It was the kind where you learn how thin the walls in your house are, and where a whole town’s worth of children discover the same thing at the same time in the same terrible detail.

Millbrook does not have a hospital. What Millbrook has is a clinic that closes at five and four hundred bathrooms.

So the adults did what adults do, which is take charge of a situation they do not understand.

Ida Prewitt started calling people at one in the morning to tell them not to panic, which woke up nine households that had been sleeping fine. Mr. Jennings drove to the store in his slippers and opened up at two so people could buy ginger ale, and by three he was out of ginger ale, and by three thirty he was selling club soda and telling people it was the same thing. Aunt Carol made a pot of something with rice in it that Lena would not describe afterward. Somebody put a message on the town’s emergency phone line, which nobody in Millbrook knew existed, saying that the situation was under control and that residents should stay hydrated and await further guidance. The Mayor’s name was on it four times.

### Text page (left), verbatim

Vex had it worst and complained least. Owen catalogued his symptoms in a notebook until his handwriting stopped being handwriting. Milo, who never gets sick, got sick, and his mother sat on the edge of the bathtub with him and told him about a wedding in 1996.

Felix had the couch, a blanket, a bucket he was insulted by, and the box of VHS tapes he’d bought for two dollars at the church rummage sale in April.

He put one in around three because he couldn’t sleep. The label was written in marker and the tape was a 1987 movie called *The Nightjar*, and it opened with a man on a fire escape narrating his own life to nobody.

Felix got about forty minutes in before he fell asleep sitting up with the tape still running.

Pip did not get sick, because Pip does not get sick. She spent the whole night sitting in the Warehouse with her hands flat on her knees, listening to a town be miserable through its wires, and around four she heard something else start up underneath it.

“Something is opening,” she said out loud, to a room with nobody in it.

### Eleven Bedrooms

The first one was on Larkspur.

A door appeared in the wall of a nine-year-old’s bedroom, about four and a half feet tall, where no door had been. It opened. A three-foot robot came through it, gray and scuffed and dented at one hip, with a spray of stiff blond hair coming off the top of his head like somebody had glued a paintbrush up there.

He had a clipboard.

“Sorry,” Chet said. “Sorry. Go back to sleep, this takes about four seconds.”

The kid did not go back to sleep. The kid watched the whole thing, and told everybody about it at school for the rest of his life, and nobody ever believed him.

Chet worked off the county health roster, because a roster is a document and a document tells you where people are. Ninety-one names in the order the county had typed them, which was not alphabetical and not by street, so his route made no sense and he walked a lot of it.

He did Cassie Reyner at four fifteen. She woke up while he was standing there and she wasn’t frightened, because she’d already had a night like this once before and nobody had believed her either.

### The Best Morning In Years

By nine o’clock every child in Millbrook was completely well.

Not recovering. Well. Ninety-one kids who had been on a bathroom floor at three in the morning were eating breakfast at nine, and every one of them said the same thing about a small man with a clipboard, and every single adult in town decided, all at once and without discussing it, that this had been a twenty-four hour bug.

Ida Prewitt said it was the humidity. Mr. Jennings said it was the club soda. The town’s emergency phone line got a new message crediting swift municipal action.

Rosa Delgado closed her truck.

The crew found her at the little league field at ten, sitting on the bumper with her keys in her hand, and she’d already written the sign that said CLOSED and she hadn’t been able to make herself tape it up.

“Ninety-one children,” she said.

“Rosa.”

“In my line. From my window.”

Milo sat down on the gravel next to her, which is a thing he does that nobody else in that crew would think of.

“Your generator went out at seven for an hour,” he said. “The warmer went cold and came back up. That’s all it was, and it wasn’t a decision you made, and anybody in this town who says otherwise is somebody I’m going to have a conversation with.”

### The Roof Of The Hardware Store

There was a man standing on the parapet of Carter’s Hardware, three stories up, in a black mask across the eyes and a cap pulled low.

He looked down at the six of them for a long moment, and then he spoke, and his voice carried the whole length of Main Street.

“Millbrook sleeps. The Nightjar does not.”

“Oh,” Lena said. “He’s early today.”

“The night is long and full of small wrongs. Somebody must be awake for them. That somebody wears a mask.” He put one boot up on the ledge. “Citizens. You have been ill. The Nightjar was ill also. The Nightjar recovered, because the guilty do not rest and neither can he.”

“Come down off there,” Owen called up. “Seriously. That ledge is bad. I’ve seen the inspection.”

“The Nightjar has heard your concern. The Nightjar dismisses it.”

Then he was gone off the back of the roof in a way that involved a rope and a bicycle frame, and the sound he made landing was not a good sound, and eleven seconds later there was a thumbs up over the top of the parapet.

Everybody laughed. Rosa laughed, which was the first good thing that had happened to her all morning. Owen shook his head and said something about liability. Milo picked up his coffee.

Pip had not laughed and had not moved.

“Where is Felix?” she said.

“What?” Lena said.

“Felix. Where is he? He was sick last night, and he did not answer his phone this morning, and he is not here.”

There was a pause of about two seconds, and the pause was the worst part, because it wasn’t confused. It was patient.

“Pip,” Milo said, kindly. “Who’s Felix?”

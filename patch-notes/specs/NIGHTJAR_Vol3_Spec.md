### The Woman On The Bench

The woman in the green cardigan sits on the bench outside the library most afternoons, and she has been sitting there for as long as anybody can remember, and nobody in Millbrook has ever once asked her why.

Her name is Delia Stroud. Pip had to look it up.

“Mrs. Stroud.”

“It’s Miss, but nobody’s called me either one since about 1994.” She moved her bag off the seat. “Sit down, honey. You’re the one who hears things.”

“Yes.”

“I know. I’ve been waiting on you a while.”

Pip sat. Delia had a paperback in her lap that she wasn’t reading and a phone beside her on the bench with no buttons on it, and Pip had been listening to that phone for two years without ever knowing whose it was.

“I need to reach the robot,” Pip said.

“I figured.”

“I have tried the walls. I have tried the wires. I have said his name out loud in a room he has been in.” Pip put her hands flat on her knees. “He is not answering, and one of my friends is gone, and I am the only person who knows it.”

Delia looked at her for a while.

“Can I tell you something about that little fella?” she said. “He’s not hiding from you. He doesn’t hide. He works a list, and when the list is done he goes home and he waits for the next one.”

“Then how does anybody get on the list?”

“Same way you get a plumber, honey.” Delia patted her knee and stood up and picked up her bag. “Just break something.”

### Two In The Morning

“So we break the road,” Vex said.

“We break the road.”

“I want to say out loud that I’ve wanted to do this since I was nine.”

Owen had the rental paperwork for the jackhammer under a landscaping company that had gone out of business in 2019, which took him eleven minutes and which he described as the easiest thing he’d done all week. Lena set up two cameras. Milo stood at the top of the street with a thermos, because somebody was going to come out of a house and ask what was happening and it was better if that somebody talked to Milo first.

At two in the morning, Vex put a jackhammer into the middle of Main Street, six feet from the curb, right where the pothole used to be.

It was extremely loud.

It took her nine minutes to open a hole about the size of a bathtub, and she was good at it, and she did not stop smiling once.

### Owen’s Notebook

Owen found it that afternoon, and he found it because he was trying to prove Pip wrong.

He keeps an equipment log for the Warehouse. Everything that comes in, everything that goes out, who has it. He started it two years ago because Felix used to take things and not bring them back, except that he did not know that sentence, and could not have said it, and what he had instead was a page.

The page said, in his own handwriting, in his own numbering system:

*4/12 — receiver mount rebuilt, F. Parts from bin 3.*

*4/19 — F. has the good multimeter. Ask.*

*5/2 — F. returned multimeter (broken).*

### The Thing Pip Did

She did it at eleven that night and she did not tell anybody first, which is the part Vex never fully forgave.

Pip has a panel in her chest about the size of a paperback. Under it there is a board, and on the board there is a small square component that Chet soldered in eleven years ago and that she has never touched. She opened the panel, took the component out, put it in her jacket pocket, and closed the panel.

Then she sat down on the loading step behind the Warehouse to wait.

What happened next was not what she expected. She thought she would go quiet. Instead everything came in at once, all of it, every wire and every phone and every appliance in a nine-block radius, with nothing sorting it, and she could not tell the fridge from the streetlight from the person walking their dog on Crescent.

Vex found her at twenty past eleven, sitting on the step with her hands flat on her knees and her eyes open, saying numbers.

“Pip.”

“Twelve. Twelve. Forty-one. Twelve.”

“Pip, look at me.” Vex got down in front of her and did not touch her, because they had talked about that, and then she touched her anyway, both hands on her shoulders. “What did you do?”

Pip’s eyes came halfway back.

“I broke something real,” she said.

“There’s nothing wrong with him,” Chet said.

“There is everything wrong with him.”

“Pip, I’m telling you what I see. He matches. Whatever he’s supposed to be, that is exactly what he is, right down to the stitching, and I cannot fix a thing that isn’t broken. I don’t have a fault to work from.” He turned around. “I need the original. I need to know what he was before. Is that written down anywhere?”

“No,” Pip said. “There are nine lines in Owen’s notebook and a list on a garage wall. That is all that is left.”

“Then I can’t—”

“It is in me.”

Chet went very still.

“I remember him,” Pip said. “I am the only one who does. And you wrote me, so you can read me.”

“Pip.”

“You can read me the way you read a chart.”

“I know I can.” Chet picked his clipboard back up and held it against his chest with both hands. “I did this. I want to say that out loud in front of all three of you before we go any further. Nobody made me look at that television.”

“How long does it take?” Pip said.

“Twenty minutes. Maybe less.”

“Then that is what we are doing.”

Up on the roof of the feed store, the Nightjar swung a directional dish around to point at the school, and did not look down, and had no idea any of them were there.

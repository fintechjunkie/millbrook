“It’s a good fence,” Vex said.

### The Wardrobe Room

The basement hall smelled like floor wax and old paper. Two flashlights made two circles on the tile, and Pip didn’t use one, which stopped being unsettling around the third week you knew her.

Forty feet down on the left there was a door with a bad hinge, and behind it a short flight of concrete steps, and at the bottom of those, the wardrobe room.

It was bigger than Owen expected and a lot fuller. Thirteen steamer trunks along the back wall, numbered in white paint by a shop teacher who retired in 1991. A rack of choir robes on a pipe. Boxes of hats. A shelf of shoes lined up in pairs with every toe pointed the same direction, which Owen decided immediately was the worst thing in the room.

Trunk seven stood open with nothing in it but a folded sheet of tissue paper.

Vex put her light down in the corner, on a scuff pattern in the dust. Four sets of bare footprints headed out, and none of them came back.

Pip wasn’t looking at any of it. She stood in the middle of the room with her head tilted at the near wall, where a typed inventory sheet hung in a plastic sleeve on a nail.

“I need everybody quiet for a minute.”

“You can just read it,” Owen said. “It’s four feet away.”

“I know. I am not reading it yet. There is a hum in the north wall and I want to hear it before I know anything, because once I know something I stop hearing properly.”

“That isn’t how ears work.”

### Ten Feet Apart

Across town, the Warehouse was doing a bad job of waiting.

Milo had checked his phone eleven times in forty minutes, which for him is pacing a hole in the rug. Lena had three edits open and hadn’t made a cut in twenty-five. Felix had built two things and taken one of them back apart.

Then Felix said, “Guys. How long has that been going on?”

Up on the high shelf, Monke was sitting straight up with a granola bar in one hand and no interest in eating it. Ten feet away, on top of the router, the dragon was sitting up too, plates flat along its back, tail hanging straight down.

They were looking right at each other.

“I think it’s a contest,” Felix whispered. He got his phone out. “I’ve got five bucks on the monkey.”

“You don’t have five bucks,” Lena said.

“I have a gift card to a store that closed.”

Four minutes later he said it was boring and went back to his bench.

Twenty minutes after that, Lena looked up because the fan clicked, and neither animal had moved so much as an inch. When Owen’s text finally came in at ten to one, they were both asleep, and they’d fallen asleep at the same second. Nobody in that room ever found out who won, and Felix told people for the rest of his life that it was the monkey.

### Old Bell Road

At ten past six the next morning, Private Kyle Dunphy had the Old Bell Road stop with Sergeant Marisol Reyes forty yards back and a jaw that still ached from Thursday.

Dame Ottilie was standing in the road again.

Reyes had written it up twice already. Her report said, in the plainest language she could manage, that the obstruction could not be moved, arrested or shocked, that it had never threatened anybody, and that it had twice offered to hold somebody’s coffee. She recommended moving the checkpoint a hundred yards north, where there was no obstruction, since the obstruction never followed anybody anywhere.

Lieutenant Pace read the report. He also had two new squads, a specialist team, and a note from an admiral in the admiral’s own handwriting, so he ordered the checkpoint held.

At fourteen past, Ray Toomey came around the bend in a five-ton delivery truck doing forty.

Ray had run that route for nineteen years and had been up since four. He saw a person standing in his road and did what anybody would do, which was stand on the brakes, and then the back end started coming around on him, and then he aimed for the shoulder, where Private Kyle Dunphy was standing with a clipboard.

Ottie got between them. She set her feet and put a shoulder out, and a five-ton truck hit a suit of steel plate with a girl inside it.

The truck lost, and that was the part nobody in Millbrook ever got over.

The front end folded up like a soda can and the whole thing stopped eleven feet earlier than it had any business stopping. Ottie slid backward maybe four inches. The airbag went off, and Ray sat there in a cloud of talcum powder with both hands still on the wheel.

Ray Toomey said a word.

“That’s all right. I’m sorry about your truck. I don’t know how to pay for a truck, because nobody’s explained money to me yet.”

Dunphy had sat down on the shoulder without deciding to. Reyes got to him in nine seconds and crouched down.

“Look at me. You’re on the ground, but you’re fine.”

“She got in front of it.”

“She did.”

“Sergeant, she got in front of a truck for me, and I’ve been pointing a checkpoint at her for two days. That’s the worst I’ve ever felt about anything.”

She stood up and took stock. A folded-up delivery truck. A girl in armor apologizing to a man in an airbag. And eleven civilians on the far shoulder with their phones up, at six in the morning, on a road that officially had nobody on it, guarded by a unit that was officially in a soybean field in Georgia.

“They’re going to hate this,” Reyes said, to nobody at all.

### Four In The Warehouse

They ended up at the Warehouse, because there was nowhere else to put them and because Vex went and got them.

It was the strangest afternoon in the building’s history, and that building has housed a crocodile.

Ottie couldn’t get the helmet off, because the impact had jammed the visor and bent something behind the jaw. Felix went at it for two hours with tin snips, a rubber mallet and a hair dryer, talking the entire time, while Ottie sat perfectly still on a milk crate and answered every question he asked her.

“Does that pinch?”

“Yes.”

“Bad pinch or regular pinch?”

“I have nothing to compare it to. This is my fourth day.”

“Right. Sorry. Okay, hold still, because this either works or it sets your hair on fire.”

“I don’t believe I have hair.”

“Then we’ll find out together.”

Rocco found the fighting game and was terrible at it, and he apologized to the television twice. Owen tried to explain the controller, and Rocco held it like a bird.
